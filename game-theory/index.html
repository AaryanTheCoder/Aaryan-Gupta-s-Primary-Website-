Experiment 03 · Prediction

Newcomb’s Paradox

A nearly perfect supercomputer has already predicted your choice—and filled the mystery box accordingly. The money is already there.

PREDICTION LOCKED ••••••••

The offer

Box A is transparent: $1,000.
Box B is opaque: $0 or $1,000,000.

If the computer predicted you’d take only B, it put $1 million in B. If it predicted you’d take both, B is empty. It is right 90% of the time.

$1K BOX A transparent
? $1M BOX B opaque
$

The boxes are open

You took home $1,000,000.

One-box expected value

$900,000 (0.90 × $1,000,000) + (0.10 × $0)

Follow the evidence: your choice strongly tracks what the accurate predictor put in B.

VS

Two-box expected value

$101,000 (0.90 × $1,000) + (0.10 × $1,001,000)

Follow dominance: the money is fixed, so taking A always adds another $1,000.

That’s the paradox: one-boxing maximises expected value when prediction accuracy matters; two-boxing seems to dominate after the boxes are already filled. Rational thinkers still disagree.

That is far higher than random guessing.

A 12-box miniature

Follow the loop—not random boxes.

Prisoner 1 opens box 1. The slip inside points to the next box. They follow that chain until they find slip 1 or use half the boxes.

Waiting for prisoner 1…

Random strategy

(½)100 ≈ 7.89 × 10−31

Every prisoner independently has a 50% chance. Multiply ½ one hundred times: effectively zero.

Loop strategy

1 − (1/51 + 1/52 + … + 1/100) = 31.18%

They succeed exactly when the random permutation contains no loop longer than 50.

Don’t take the formula’s word for it
